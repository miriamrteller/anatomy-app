1. The Cranium (8 Bones)
The "brain box" designed for protection and housing sensory organs.

Frontal Bone
Os frontale
A thick, shell-like bone forming the anterior roof of the cranium and the forehead. It contains the frontal sinuses and forms the superior margin of the orbits (eye sockets). It articulates with the parietal, sphenoid, ethmoid, and nasal bones via the coronal and other sutures.
Parietal Bones (2)
Os parietale
Large, quadrilateral bones that form the bulk of the cranial roof and sides. They articulate with each other at the sagittal suture, the frontal bone at the coronal suture, and the occipital bone at the lambdoid suture. They protect the parietal lobes of the brain.
Temporal Bones (2)
Os temporale
Complex bones at the lateral base of the skull. They house the structures of the inner and middle ear (auditory ossicles). Key landmarks include the zygomatic process, the mastoid process, and the external auditory meatus. They articulate with the mandible at the TMJ.
Occipital Bone
Os occipitale
Forms the posterior and inferior base of the neurocranium. It contains the foramen magnum, the large opening for the spinal cord. It features the occipital condyles, which articulate with the Atlas (C1) to allow for nodding motions.
Sphenoid Bone
Os sphenoidale
Known as the "keystone" of the cranial floor because it articulates with all other cranial bones. It is butterfly-shaped, containing the sella turcica (which holds the pituitary gland) and the optic canals for the optic nerves.
Ethmoid Bone
Os ethmoidale
A light, spongy bone located between the orbits at the roof of the nose. It forms part of the anterior cranial fossa, the medial walls of the orbits, and the nasal septum. It contains the cribriform plate, which allows olfactory nerves to pass to the brain.

Vertebrae cervicales
The seven smallest vertebrae forming the neck (C1–C7). They feature transverse foramina for vertebral artery passage. C1 (Atlas) lacks a body and supports the skull; C2 (Axis) features the dens (odontoid process) for head rotation.

Twelve mid-back vertebrae (T1–T12) characterized by heart-shaped bodies and long, downward-sloping spinous processes. They feature costal facets for articulation with the ribs and provide stability to the thoracic cage.

The "tailbone," typically formed by 3 to 5 fused rudimentary vertebrae. It represents the terminal end of the vertebral column and serves as an attachment site for various pelvic floor muscles and ligaments.

Batch 2: The Facial Bones (14 Bones)
Focus: Structural support for features, airway protection, and mastication.
Name

Comprehensive AI-Ready Description
Maxilla (2)
Maxilla
The upper jawbones that form the central facial skeleton. They house the upper teeth within the alveolar process, form the floor of the orbits, and the lateral walls of the nasal cavity. Each maxilla contains a large maxillary sinus. They articulate with every facial bone except the mandible, providing the rigid foundation for the mid-face.
Mandible
Mandibula
The largest, strongest, and only movable bone of the skull. It consists of a horizontal body housing the lower teeth and two vertical rami. The condylar process articulates with the temporal bone (TMJ), while the coronoid process serves as a lever for the temporalis muscle during chewing. It is the primary structural component of the lower face.
Zygomatic (2)
Os zygomaticum
The "cheekbones" forming the lateral wall and floor of the orbits. They articulate with the frontal, sphenoid, temporal, and maxillary bones. They provide the structural "corners" of the face and serve as the origin for the masseter muscle, essential for powerful jaw closure.
Nasal Bones (2)
Os nasale
Two small, oblong bones placed side-by-side that form the bridge of the nose. While small, they provide the primary rigid support for the superior portion of the nose; the remainder of the nasal structure is composed of flexible hyaline cartilage.
Lacrimal (2)
Os lacrimale
The smallest and most fragile facial bones, located in the medial wall of each orbit. They feature the lacrimal sulcus, which houses the lacrimal sac and nasolacrimal duct, allowing tears to drain from the eye surface into the nasal cavity.
Palatine (2)
Os palatinum
L-shaped bones located at the posterior of the nasal cavity. They form the posterior third of the hard palate (roof of the mouth), part of the nasal floor, and a tiny portion of the orbital floor. Failure of these bones to fuse correctly during development can contribute to a cleft palate.
Inferior Nasal Conchae (2)
Concha nasalis inferior
Thin, curved, scroll-like bones projecting from the lateral walls of the nasal cavity. Unlike the superior/middle conchae (part of the ethmoid), these are independent bones. They function to create turbulence in inhaled air, increasing surface area for warming, moistening, and filtering.
Vomer
Vomer
A thin, flat, plow-shaped bone situated in the midsagittal line. It articulates with the ethmoid bone and the maxillae/palatines to form the inferior and posterior portion of the bony nasal septum, which provides the primary structural division of the nasal airway.

Malleus (2)
Malleus
The "hammer"; the largest of the middle ear ossicles. The "handle" is attached to the internal surface of the tympanic membrane (eardrum), while the head articulates with the incus. It converts sound waves into mechanical vibrations.

Sternum
Sternum
The "breastbone"; a flat bone in the center of the chest. Costae fluctuantes
Rib pairs 11 and 12. They articulate posteriorly with T11 and T12 vertebrae but have no anterior attachment. They are capped with cartilage and embedded in the abdominal wall, providing protection for the kidneys while allowing maximum torso flexibility.

Vertebrae cervicales

The "tailbone"; a small, triangular bone formed by the fusion of 3 to 5 rudimentary vertebrae. While it is a vestigial tail, it serves as a critical attachment point for the levator ani muscles and various ligaments that support the pelvic floor.

Scapula
The "shoulder blade"; a flat, triangular bone on the posterolateral aspect of the thorax (ribs 2-7). Humerus

Radius
The lateral bone of the forearm, situated on the thumb side. Its disc-shaped head articulates with the humerus and the radial notch of the ulna. Ulna (2)
Ulna
The medial bone of the forearm (pinky side). It is longer than the radius and acts as the stabilizing bone. Carpals (16)
Ossa carpi
Eight small, irregular bones per wrist arranged in two rows. Proximal row: Scaphoid, Lunate, Triquetrum, Pisiform. Distal row: Trapezium, Trapezoid, Capitate, Hamate. Metacarpals (10)
Ossa metacarpi
Five miniature long bones per hand forming the framework of the palm. They are numbered 1 (thumb) to 5 (pinky). Each consists of a base (proximal), a shaft, and a head (distal), the latter of which forms the "knuckles" when the hand is clenched. They provide the structural span between the wrist and the fingers.
Phalanges (Hand) (28)
Phalanges digitorum manus
The bones of the fingers. Each finger (2-5) contains three phalanges: proximal, middle, and distal. The thumb (pollex) contains only two: proximal and distal. These bones provide the leverage needed for both "power grips" and the "precision grip" required for fine motor tasks.

Batch 7: The Pelvic Girdle & Lower Limbs (62 Bones)
Focus: Weight-bearing, stability, and locomotion.
Name
Latin Name
Comprehensive AI-Ready Description
Hip Bone (2)
Os coxae
Also called the coxal or pelvic bone; a large, irregular bone formed by the fusion of the Ilium (superior flare), Ischium (inferior posterior "sit bone"), and Pubis (anterior). The three meet at the acetabulum, the deep socket for the femur. The two hip bones articulate anteriorly at the pubic symphysis and posteriorly with the sacrum to form the bony pelvis.
Femur (2)
Femur
The thigh bone; the longest, heaviest, and strongest bone in the body. Its spherical head fits into the acetabulum, while its neck—a common site for fractures—projects the shaft laterally. Distally, the medial and lateral condyles articulate with the tibia and patella. It is the primary load-bearing bone of the lower body.
Patella (2)
Patella
The "kneecap"; a large, flat sesamoid bone triangular in shape. It is situated within the quadriceps femoris tendon and articulates with the patellar surface of the femur. Its primary function is to increase the mechanical advantage (leverage) of the quadriceps muscle during leg extension and to protect the knee joint.
Tibia (2)
Tibia
The "shinbone"; the second-longest bone in the body and the primary weight-bearing bone of the lower leg. It articulates proximally with the femoral condyles to form the knee and distally with the talus to form the ankle. Its medial malleolus forms the inner prominence of the ankle.
Fibula (2)
Fibula
A slender, non-weight-bearing bone on the lateral side of the tibia. Its proximal head does not reach the knee joint, but its distal end, the lateral malleolus, forms the outer prominence of the ankle and provides critical lateral stability to the talocrural (ankle) joint. It serves as a major origin point for leg muscles.
Tarsals (14)
Ossa tarsi
Seven bones per ankle/foot: Talus (articulates with tibia), Calcaneus (the heel bone), Navicular, Cuboid, and three Cuneiforms (medial, intermediate, lateral). These bones are larger and stronger than carpals to support the body's entire weight and facilitate the distribution of force during walking.
Metatarsals (10)
Ossa metatarsi
Five bones forming the arch and midfoot. Like the hand, they are numbered 1 (big toe) to 5. The first metatarsal is significantly thicker as it plays a major role in the "toe-off" phase of walking. They provide the structural bridge between the ankle and the toes.
Phalanges (Foot) (28)
Phalanges digitorum pedis
The bones of the toes. The big toe (hallux) has two phalanges: proximal and distal. The other four toes have three: proximal, middle, and distal. While smaller than those in the hand, they are vital for maintaining balance and Providing propulsion during the gait cycle.
